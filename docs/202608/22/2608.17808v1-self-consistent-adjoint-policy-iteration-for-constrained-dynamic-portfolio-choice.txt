Abstract 

We develop simulation-based policy iteration for continuous-time portfolio choice with predictable returns and convex constraints. Each outer step re-evaluates a fixed-latent OL-BPTT adjoint after deployment and solves the constrained update. Shifted-adjoint cancellation controls the adjoint–HJB Hamiltonian-gradient discrepancy by the policy-improvement residual. For CRRA portfolios, exact HJB policy iteration identifies the optimal reduced value factor, while population OL-BPTT iteration converges globally under an occupation-measure relative-error condition. A theorem-matched audit yields a maximal 95% upper endpoint of 0 .074 against the required 0 .75 threshold. In a three-factor, fifty-asset design, current-policy re-evaluation outperforms matched pooled refinement under both evaluation laws. 

Keywords: dynamic portfolio choice; stochastic control; policy iteration; adjoint methods; portfolio constraints.

# 1 Introduction 

Continuous-time portfolio choice with stochastic investment opportunities is analytically tractable only in a limited set of unconstrained models. Classical results characterize optimal investment through dynamic programming, martingale duality, or explicit affine solutions [4, 18, 23, 24, 35, 37]. Once short-sale, borrowing, or asset-wise position limits are imposed, the portfolio rule develops active regions and switching boundaries. With several return-predicting factors, a direct Hamilton– Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) discretization also becomes expensive even though the portfolio action itself remains a convex quadratic problem. Simulation-based dynamic portfolio methods avoid a full factor grid [2], and recent financial algorithms combine stochastic approximation or reinforcement learning with convergence and error analysis [3, 33]. More broadly, continuous-time learning methods estimate value, q-function, actor–critic, or policy-gradient objects [14, 29, 34]. Their numerical bottleneck is often a repeatedly trained global approximator. The present paper follows a different route: it learns only the low-dimensional continuation statistic needed to recover the portfolio action, and it solves the high-dimensional constrained action block explicitly. Two iterative traditions provide the closest numerical context. Stochastic methods of successive approximations alternate the forward state, Pontryagin adjoint equations, and Hamiltonian control

2.5 Policy dependence and the matched-budget comparator 

Deploying an updated portfolio changes the law under which the next continuation field is evaluated. A second estimate under the initial policy therefore need not target the same policy-improvement operator as a current-policy re-evaluation. The experiments compare these two uses of a matched simulation budget; this comparison tests policy dependence but is not an assumption of the convergence results.

Remark 3.2 (Scope of the generic theorem) . The result upgrades the generic consistency layer from a first-adjoint statement to the adjoint–HJB Hamiltonian-gradient discrepancy at the updated control used by the value-improvement bound. It does not verify the gain-dependent pointwise or occupation-measure relative error conditions introduced in the next subsection: the right side of Equation (51) is a future-tube supremum with a local constant that need not be small. Localization to the updated control is essential rather than cosmetic. At a fixed point, ( P u − Γu)( σ(a) − σ(u)) need not vanish for actions far from u, so uniform-over-action convergence of the Hamiltonian gradient is generally false without P u → Γu. No claim is made across switching surfaces, moving feasible fibers, degenerate regions lacking an interior gradient estimate, or singular controls. The CRRA realization below is stronger computationally: homotheticity removes the shifted and second-adjoint blocks from the represented Hamiltonian altogether.

4.3 Local acceleration after active-face identification 

The preceding results are global but rate-free. The Newton interpretation of policy iteration goes back to Puterman and Brumelle [28]; more recent PDE and neural policy-iteration analyses obtain Newton or superlinear behavior from approximate policy evaluation [9, 11]. The relative-error condition used above and the stronger second-order forcing used below are also analogous in form to classical inexact-Newton forcing conditions [5]. Our route is different: the vanishing first derivative is obtained from fixed-face envelope cancellation of the policy-evaluation operator, and the inexactness is an adjoint–HJB Hamiltonian-gradient error rather than a linear-solve residual. Near a regular optimum, two additional facts become available. First, on a fixed active face the performance-difference identity represents the value gap as a weighted occupation norm squared rather than merely bounding it abstractly. Second, the fixed-policy evaluation operator has an envelope cancellation. The next results isolate these mechanisms. They are local to an interior region or to a pointwise active-face field that has already been identified; no rate is claimed across a switching surface. Remark 4.6 (Population OL-BPTT iteration versus sampled computation) . Theorems 4.7 and 4.9 establish the global target for the exact HJB policy iteration and for an exact population OL-BPTT iteration satisfying relative Hamiltonian-gradient error control under the occupation measure. The latter assumes an exact maximizer of the population OL-BPTT adjoint Hamiltonian and is not automatically a finite-sample theorem. A realized numerical sequence may still fall under the same argument if its total adjoint–HJB Hamiltonian-gradient discrepancy and local-solver error satisfy an appropriate occupation-measure relative error condition together with the uniform local regularity bound. Otherwise a fixed sampled implementation need not converge exactly to u⋆ when adjoint–HJB inconsistency, time discretization, conditional estimation, sampling, representation, continuation-extension, or KKT errors persist. Such a computation is covered by the a posteriori value-improvement bound. Even in a representation-closed benchmark, consistency of the adjoint-field estimator at each fixed outer iteration gives only fixed-iteration consistency; a joint limit with Nout → ∞ , Mk → ∞ , and hk → 0 additionally requires iteration-uniform stability and a vanishing-error schedule.

Protocol and terminology. The generic per-update template—fixed-latent OL-BPTT adjoint estimation, conditional reconstruction, and an explicit local control solve—is inherited from the companion, while the reduced CRRA field and Hamiltonian used in the constrained experiments are the present specialization. The experiments focus on how the resulting policy-improvement operator is re-evaluated and averaged across outer policies; one-shot accuracy or action-block scalability alone is not the claimed novelty. The constrained experiments use CRRA-reduced fixed-latent OL-BPTT throughout. The adjoint– HJB consistency audit first checks the corrected HJB reference and then measures the finite-iteration OL-BPTT defect. The physical estimator uses self-normalized terminal wealth-power weights. Its Girsanov-tilted counterpart uses the equivalent reduced Feynman–Kac representation: with 

χ = 1 −γ, the factor drift is shifted from KZ (¯ z −z) to KZ (¯ z −z)+ χC ⊤u, while the policy-dependent running potential is χ{u⊤μ(z) − γu ⊤Σu/ 2} up to the policy-independent risk-free term. Thus tilting changes the sampling law, not the fixed-policy target. The common-random-number finite difference (CRN finite difference) perturbs an anchor state in opposite directions using the same Brownian increments. It is used only as an audit: a centered finite difference for a dz -dimensional field requires paired perturbed continuation banks for every factor coordinate and anchor, whereas OL-BPTT acquires all state-gradient components in one reverse pass through each rollout. For an estimated field ̂ r and corrected target r⋆ over the audit restart points, the reported calibration slope is the zero-intercept least-squares coefficient ⟨̂r, r ⋆⟩ / ∥r⋆∥2; unit slope indicates correct amplitude. An initial legacy HJB reference used the wrong upwind direction for the drift in forward time-to-maturity. The independent CRN finite-difference audit exposed the resulting factor-of-two discrepancy. After correcting the stencil, the finite-difference slope is near one, while fixed-latent 36 OL-BPTT retains a resolved finite-iteration consistency error at the tested policy. At 131 ,072 paths, the physical-measure slopes are 1 .0154 for CRN finite difference and 1 .0621 for OL-BPTT; under the tilted measure they are 1 .0146 and 1 .0618, respectively. The tilted relative L2 errors are 1 .48% for finite difference and 6 .66% for OL-BPTT. Tilting increases the median effective-sample-size fraction from 0 .645 to 0 .936 and reduces the top-one-percent weight share from 10 .1% to 2 .24%. Thus additional paths and a better sampling law reduce uncertainty but do not remove the population-level adjoint–HJB consistency defect at a fixed policy. # 6 Discussion 

Financial interpretation. The learned object is the continuation hedging field rather than an end-to-end portfolio network. Under CRRA preferences, the many-asset action block is decoded by an explicit quadratic program once that field is known. Re-evaluation matters when the implemented portfolio changes the distribution of investment opportunities and hence the hedging demand. The Merton experiment is the negative control: the HJB policy-improvement operator is policy independent and concentrated one-shot estimation is preferable. With predictable returns, the current-policy improvement operator contains information that cannot be recovered by spending the same budget on repeated simulations under the initial feedback. 

Constraints, coverage, and decision relevance. Portfolio constraints turn factor space into lower-bound, interior, and upper-bound regions. The relevant object is therefore not only average policy error but also the location of active boundaries. Averaging the pre-projection control field and projecting only after interpolation preserves this geometry. Current-policy sampling naturally concentrates on the induced occupation measure, but on-policy design points alone may miss financially relevant tail states. The final fifty–fifty mixture of on-policy and broad stationary anchors improves broad and tail accuracy while preserving the on-policy accuracy advantage of re-evaluation. 

Convergence and numerical design. For strongly concave quadratic HJB Hamiltonians, the exact HJB policy-improvement operator has a damped improvement certificate and residual summability. At the generic level, shifted-adjoint cancellation shows that the second PMP adjoint need not approach the value Hessian: on a regular future tube its discrepancy is multiplied by updated-control diffusion displacement, and the adjoint–HJB Hamiltonian-gradient discrepancy decays proportionally to the policy-improvement residual. This local structural result does not make the relative constant small. A gain-dependent occupation-measure relative error condition 40 therefore remains the hypothesis that transfers value improvement to the population OL-BPTT portfolio iteration. A fixed half step permits the sufficient threshold κ < 3/4, whereas admitting full steps requires κ < 1/2; damping enlarges the tolerated ratio but does not remove the population adjoint–HJB discrepancy. In the theorem-matched one-factor audit, the largest per-bank 95% upper endpoint is 0 .073936 over 180 audited combinations, providing substantial empirical margin for the half-step threshold on that visited sequence. At finite budget, time discretization, sampling law, representation, damping, and design-point coverage remain distinct design choices. Richardson extrapolation is useful in a discretization-dominated one-factor regime but can lose when multifactor regression noise dominates. 

Limitations. Global convergence is proved for the constrained CRRA population subclass. The generic adjoint–HJB consistency theorem is local to regular future tubes and does not verify the iterate-uniform occupation-measure relative error constant required by the OL-BPTT global theorem. The new theorem-matched audit verifies the threshold only on the visited one-factor benchmark sequence and listed starting states. Local quadratic rates assume active-face identification, and coercivity of the weighted occupation norm is used only for value-gap conversion. No dimension-free factor-state complexity or exact convergence of a fixed biased sampled implementation is claimed. 

# 7 Conclusion 

We closed a fixed-latent OL-BPTT/Pontryagin adjoint-to-control map into a self-consistent policy iteration that re-estimates a low-dimensional hedging field under the deployed portfolio and recovers the constrained many-asset action explicitly. Shifted-adjoint cancellation yields a residual bound for the adjoint–HJB Hamiltonian-gradient discrepancy on regular future tubes without identifying the second PMP adjoint with the value Hessian. For constrained CRRA portfolios, population OL-BPTT policy iteration converges globally under an occupation-measure relative error condition. After regular active-face identification, exact full-step HJB policy iteration is locally quadratic, and the full-step population OL-BPTT iteration inherits this rate under a quadratic operator-accuracy condition. A theorem-matched benchmark audit gives a largest per-bank 95% upper confidence endpoint of 0 .073936 against the half-step threshold 0 .75. Under the selected three-factor, fifty-asset 50–50 design, re-evaluation outperforms matched pooled initial-policy refinement under both on-policy and broad evaluation laws in all three seeds.
